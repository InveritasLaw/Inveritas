The Inversion Brief — Issue #003

Three Crimes Hiding in One Texas Assault Statute

Texas Penal Code § 22.01 is not one crime. It's three — each with different mental state requirements, different burdens of proof, and different built-in defenses the legislature wrote directly into the code. Most defendants never realize which version they're actually charged under.

April 10, 2026 13 min read Inveritas Research
Statutory Inversion Texas Assault Defense § 22.01 Self-Defense

When someone says "I got charged with assault," they could be describing three completely different criminal offenses — with three different mental state requirements, three different punishment ranges, and three different sets of available defenses. The Texas assault statute bundles them all under one section number, and most people never realize which version they're actually facing.

This matters because the defense strategy that works for one version of assault can be completely irrelevant to another. Inverting § 22.01 means first identifying which of the three offenses is actually being charged, then attacking the specific elements the prosecution must prove for that version.

The Statute: Three Offenses, One Section

§ 22.01(a) — Assault

"A person commits an offense if the person:

(1) intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly causes bodily injury to another, including the person's spouse;

(2) intentionally or knowingly threatens another with imminent bodily injury, including the person's spouse; or

(3) intentionally or knowingly causes physical contact with another when the person knows or should reasonably believe that the other will regard the contact as offensive or provocative." Tex. Penal Code § 22.01(a)

Three subsections. Three different crimes. Look at the mental state requirements — they're not the same across all three:

Bodily Injury

Class A Misdemeanor
(a)(2)
Threat

Class C Misdemeanor
(a)(3)
Offensive Contact
Intentionally or knowingly only
Class C Misdemeanor

The difference between a Class A misdemeanor (up to one year in jail, $4,000 fine) and a Class C misdemeanor (fine only, no jail) can come down to a single word in the charging instrument. And the mental state requirements — the words that determine whether the prosecution can even reach the jury — are different for each version.

Key Inversion Principle

Before attacking any element of an assault charge, identify which subsection you're charged under. The mental state the prosecution must prove, the conduct they must demonstrate, and the defenses available to you are all determined by which of the three offenses is in the charging instrument. A defense that defeats (a)(1) may be irrelevant to (a)(3), and vice versa.

Six Inversion Vectors

V1 DEFINITIONAL
"Bodily Injury" Means Almost Nothing — And That's the Problem
Texas Penal Code § 1.07(a)(8) defines "bodily injury" as "physical pain, illness, or any impairment of physical condition." The threshold is remarkably low — any physical pain qualifies. But this low threshold cuts both ways. The prosecution still must prove that bodily injury actually occurred. In many assault cases, the alleged victim's testimony is the only evidence of injury — no photos, no medical records, no visible marks. If the defense can establish that no physical pain actually resulted from the contact, the bodily injury element fails and the charge under (a)(1) collapses. The prosecution cannot bootstrap an (a)(1) charge from conduct that, at most, constitutes offensive contact under (a)(3) — a dramatically less serious offense.
Tex. Penal Code § 1.07(a)(8); Tex. Penal Code § 22.01(a)(1)
V2 DEFINITIONAL
The Mens Rea Gap: "Recklessly" Only Applies to Bodily Injury
This is the most overlooked inversion in the entire statute. Subsection (a)(1) — bodily injury — allows conviction for reckless conduct. But subsections (a)(2) and (a)(3) — threat and offensive contact — require the state to prove the defendant acted intentionally or knowingly. Reckless conduct is not sufficient for a threat or offensive contact charge. This means that if the evidence shows the defendant's conduct was reckless but not intentional or knowing — an accidental shove, an unintentional bump, a careless gesture — the state cannot convict under (a)(2) or (a)(3). And for (a)(1), "recklessly" has a specific statutory definition under § 6.03(c): the defendant must have been "aware of but consciously disregard[ed] a substantial and unjustifiable risk." Mere negligence, stupidity, or thoughtlessness is not recklessness. The prosecution must prove conscious disregard — that the defendant actually foresaw the risk and chose to ignore it.
Tex. Penal Code § 22.01(a)(1)–(3); Tex. Penal Code § 6.03(c)
V3 STATUTORY
Self-Defense: The Statute the Legislature Built Into the Code
Texas Penal Code § 9.31 provides that a person is justified in using force against another when and to the degree the actor reasonably believes the force is immediately necessary to protect against the other's use or attempted use of unlawful force. Texas is a stand-your-ground state — there is no duty to retreat before using force in self-defense (§ 9.32(c)). Once the defendant raises self-defense and produces some evidence supporting it, the burden shifts to the prosecution to disprove self-defense beyond a reasonable doubt. The prosecution must prove that the defendant did not reasonably believe force was necessary. This is not a technicality — it is one of the most powerful structural advantages in Texas criminal law. The standard is the defendant's reasonable belief, not whether the threat was objectively real.
Tex. Penal Code §§ 9.31, 9.32(c); Saxton v. State, 804 S.W.2d 910 (Tex. Crim. App. 1991)
V4 STATUTORY
The Consent Defense: Mutual Combat Is Written Into the Law
Texas Penal Code § 22.06 provides that the victim's effective consent — or the actor's reasonable belief that the victim consented — is a defense to assault if the conduct did not threaten or inflict serious bodily injury, or if the victim knew the conduct was a risk of their occupation, recognized medical treatment, or a scientific experiment. In plain language: if both parties agreed to fight, and nobody suffered "serious" bodily injury (which is a much higher threshold than regular bodily injury — it means substantial risk of death, permanent disfigurement, or protracted loss of function), the consent defense applies. Many bar fights, mutual altercations, and physical confrontations involve some degree of mutual engagement. The prosecution must prove that the alleged victim did not consent — and in ambiguous situations, that burden is harder to carry than it looks.
Tex. Penal Code § 22.06; Tex. Penal Code § 1.07(a)(46) (definition of "serious bodily injury")
V5 TIER CONFLICT
The Family Violence Enhancement Trap
Identical conduct — the same push, the same shove, the same contact — can be charged as a Class C misdemeanor or a Class A misdemeanor based solely on the relationship between the parties. If the prosecution adds a "family violence" designation under § 71.004 of the Family Code, the consequences explode: a second family violence assault becomes a third-degree felony (2–10 years in prison), a conviction triggers a lifetime federal firearms prohibition under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(9), and deferred adjudication does not prevent the conviction from being used as a prior for enhancement purposes. The family violence designation depends on whether the parties had a dating relationship, lived together, or are related by blood or marriage. The definition of "dating relationship" under Family Code § 71.0021 is broad and subjective. Challenging the relationship characterization can mean the difference between a fine-only offense and a felony with federal consequences.
Tex. Penal Code § 22.01(b); Tex. Fam. Code §§ 71.003, 71.004, 71.0021; 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(9)
V6 EVIDENTIARY
The Single-Witness Problem
A significant percentage of assault cases — particularly domestic assault cases — rest entirely on the testimony of one person: the alleged victim. There is no corroboration requirement in Texas, meaning a conviction can rest on a single witness's uncorroborated testimony. But the defense is not without tools. Prior inconsistent statements, motive to fabricate (custody disputes, divorce proceedings, immigration leverage), recantation patterns, and the absence of any physical evidence all go to credibility. Texas Rule of Evidence 613 allows impeachment with prior inconsistent statements. If the complainant made a 911 call or gave a statement to police that differs from their trial testimony, those inconsistencies are admissible. And under Crawford v. Washington (2004), the defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to confront and cross-examine the witness — if the complainant does not appear at trial, their prior statements may be inadmissible as testimonial hearsay.
Tex. R. Evid. 613; Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004); Tex. Const. Art. I, § 10

What This Means If You're Facing an Assault Charge

First, identify which subsection you're charged under. Read the charging instrument — the information or complaint — word by word. Is the state alleging bodily injury (a)(1), threat (a)(2), or offensive contact (a)(3)? The answer determines your entire defense strategy.

Second, if self-defense applies, raise it early. Once you produce some evidence of self-defense — your testimony alone can be sufficient — the burden shifts to the prosecution to disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt. This is a structural advantage you cannot afford to waive by failing to assert it.

Third, watch for the family violence designation. If the charging instrument includes "family violence" language, the collateral consequences are severe and permanent — including a federal firearms prohibition that survives even a deferred adjudication. Challenge the relationship characterization if the facts support it.

Fourth, demand all evidence. Body camera footage, 911 recordings, photographs (or the absence of photographs), medical records (or the absence of medical records), and all prior statements by the complainant. The gap between what the complainant told the 911 operator and what they tell the jury is often where assault cases collapse.

The Broader Point

One statute section. Three separate offenses. Three different mental states. Built-in affirmative defenses for self-defense and consent. A relationship-based enhancement that can turn a fine-only offense into a felony with permanent federal consequences. And an evidentiary landscape where the entire prosecution may rest on a single person's word.
